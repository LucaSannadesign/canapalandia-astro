*A monumental tomb, cannabis seeds and molecular traces in human hair. Research published in Science on 5 November 2026 pushes back the timeline of evidence for psychoactive cannabis use — while leaving some important questions open.*

There is a certain irony in the thought that modern societies still argue about cannabis as if it were a recent invention, when people in the Siberian steppe may have known its mind-altering effects 2,800 years ago.

No coffee shops, vaporizers or television debates about THC percentages. There were horses, elaborate funerals and the **Scythians**, a horse-riding culture that shaped the history of Eurasia.

The new evidence comes from a study led by archaeologist **Gino Caspari** of the University of Bern and the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology, published in *Science* on 5 November 2026.

## Tunnug 1: an extraordinary burial in southern Siberia

**Tunnug 1** is a large burial mound in the Uyuk Valley in Tuva, southern Siberia. It belongs to the Scythian cultural world and is associated with high-status burials.

Archaeologists recovered **about 1,000 cannabis seeds**. Six were directly radiocarbon dated, placing their deposition in the early eighth century BCE.

Seeds on their own do not prove intoxication. Cannabis has long been used for food, oil and fibre, and a plant placed in a grave might simply have been an offering.

The more unusual evidence came from **human hair preserved in the central burial chamber**.

## Cannabinoids in ancient hair — what does that prove?

Using methods including high-resolution mass spectrometry, researchers found clear signals consistent with **cannabinol (CBN)** in several hair samples. CBN can form when **THC** oxidises. Weaker signals were also consistent with THC.

The authors interpret the combination of seeds and molecular traces as evidence that cannabis was used for its intoxicating properties. They describe it as the oldest directly dated evidence of this kind currently known.

However, the findings do not determine the amount consumed, the THC concentration in the plants or the route of exposure. They do not establish whether anyone smoked, inhaled vapour or ingested cannabis.

This is not a discovery of the first joint in history. It is a scientific argument built from botanical and chemical evidence, with clear limits.

## Herodotus, hot stones and Scythian rituals

Writing in the fifth century BCE, the Greek historian **Herodotus** described Scythians putting cannabis on hot stones beneath covered structures, producing vapour and excited responses.

Ancient narratives are not laboratory reports. But archaeology allows researchers to examine whether accounts of this kind are consistent with material remains.

In **2019**, a *Science Advances* study identified cannabinoid residues in wooden braziers at **Jirzankal** in the Pamir Mountains of western China. Dating to around **2,500 years ago**, the finds supported cannabis burning during mortuary rituals.

The Siberian burial may push the direct record of psychoactive use back by around three centuries. That does not mean the Scythians invented it; it means this is where some of the oldest direct evidence has survived.

## A funerary setting, not a modern lifestyle

The Tunnug 1 burials were accompanied by large-scale horse sacrifices and indications of possible human sacrifice. Within that context, cannabis may have been part of funerary rituals.

Who used it remains uncertain. Was it a priestly practice, a communal ceremony or something associated with the deceased? Was it common or restricted to elites?

Calling it simply recreational use would impose a modern label on an ancient society. In a ritual of death and transition, altered consciousness could have held meanings very different from those familiar today.

## The scientific disagreement: use or external contamination?

Some independent researchers believe the claims require stronger evidence.

Forensic toxicologist **Volker Auwärter** of the University of Freiburg has argued that the hair analyses may not sufficiently distinguish consumption from external contamination. Hair buried alongside cannabis might carry molecular traces even without ingestion.

Archaeologist **Elisa Guerra Doce** of the University of Valladolid takes a more positive view, noting that toxicological hair analysis is an established approach in archaeological research on psychoactive substances.

The distinction matters. We have an intriguing interpretation supported by physical evidence, but not a unanimous scientific verdict.

## Cannabis has a much longer history than our headlines

Humans have used cannabis for fibre, food, traditional medicine and probably mind-altering rituals across many centuries. Tunnug 1 adds an important piece to that history.

It does not prove anything about the safety of modern cannabis products, nor does it settle medical or legal arguments. **Ancient use is not evidence of harmlessness.**

Researchers are now investigating DNA preserved in the plant remains, hoping to learn more about cannabis domestication and dispersal.

People in southern Siberia may have known its psychoactive effects 2,800 years ago. We are still learning how to discuss the plant without reducing every finding to a modern political slogan.
