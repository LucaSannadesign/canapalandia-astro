Europe’s industrial hemp sector has expanded, but the EU still lacks a fully uniform framework for every part of the plant. That is the central message of “Industrial hemp sector in the EU”, a briefing updated on 16 November 2026 by the European Parliamentary Research Service. It brings together figures, applications and rules; it does not create new law or automatically change national requirements.

## A useful snapshot, not an automatic green light

The briefing covers fibres, hurds, seeds and other plant components used in construction, textiles, food, feed and bio-based materials. It reports 37,830 hectares of fibre hemp in the EU in 2025, up from 20,540 hectares in 2015. It also warns that sector data remain incomplete because statistics do not always capture hemp grown for seed or land cultivated outside CAP support.

One internal inconsistency deserves attention. The summary refers to approximately 197,000 tonnes, while the detailed section gives 179,020 tonnes for 2025. Without clarification from the EPRS about the summary, total output cannot be treated as a settled figure. Institutional sources still require careful reading.

## What is already in force

Under the 2023–2027 CAP, hemp areas may qualify for direct payments when certified seed from authorised varieties is used and THC does not exceed 0.3%, as set out in Regulation (EU) 2021/2115. Member States also apply the controls and record-keeping required by EU rules.

This is an agricultural and CAP-support framework. It does not mean that every plant part or derivative may be marketed freely for every use. Food, extracts, cosmetics, feed and technical products follow different rules; Novel Food requirements remain relevant for cannabinoids intended for food.

## The whole-plant file is still moving through the legislature

On 16 July 2025, the European Commission proposed extending the common market organisation (CMO) framework to all parts of the hemp plant. On 19 October 2026, the Council adopted a partial negotiating mandate within the 2028–2034 EU budget framework, retaining seed certification and the 0.3% THC threshold. It also introduced safeguards for national rules on health, drugs and public security. This is neither a final regulation nor a general authorisation to market hemp products.

For operators, the distinction matters: a Commission proposal, a Council mandate and an applicable regulation are different legal stages. Treating them as equivalent confuses agricultural eligibility with commercial compliance.

## Cultivation is only one part of the chain

The briefing highlights fragmented national rules, processing capacity concentrated in a limited number of regions and incomplete data. More hectares do not automatically create a viable value chain without processing plants, offtake agreements, standards and stable end markets.

Environmental claims also need context. Crop rotation, soil cover and long-lived products may deliver benefits, but they do not make every hemp crop or product sustainable by definition. Soil, water availability, processing, transport, product life and end-of-life management all matter.

## What the briefing changes today

It provides an updated reference point, not a regulatory shortcut. Before making a business decision, operators still need to verify the crop’s intended use, applicable national rules, final-product requirements and actual processing or purchasing capacity.

The sound conclusion is cautious: the EU increasingly recognises hemp’s industrial value, but harmonisation remains unfinished. The decisive evidence will be the final legal text, not a headline or policy summary.
