CBD is frequently linked to sleep through simple claims: it “relaxes”, “helps you fall asleep” or “improves rest”. The clinical evidence is far less straightforward. Some studies report favourable signals for individual outcomes, while others find no important difference from placebo. Much of the literature also concerns medical cannabis or mixed-cannabinoid preparations rather than cannabidiol alone.

The most up-to-date synthesis available in October 2026 calls for caution. A Cochrane review published on 13 October included nine randomised studies with 537 adults with insomnia. Compared with placebo, medical cannabis may have little to no clinically meaningful effect on sleep quality, total sleep time or daytime functioning. Certainty was very low because studies were small, heterogeneous and often at risk of bias.

This does not prove that every preparation is ineffective. It shows that current evidence cannot support the blanket promise that “CBD improves sleep”.

## At a glance

- The 2026 Cochrane review found no clear clinically meaningful benefit of medical cannabis for sleep quality or duration; certainty was very low.

- Medical cannabis does not automatically mean isolated CBD: formulations, THC content, administration routes and populations differ.

- In a small 2024 trial of 150 mg CBD nightly, most sleep outcomes did not differ from placebo.

- Research doses are not consumer instructions and cannot be transferred to retail products.

- Drowsiness, drug interactions, inconsistent product quality and safety uncertainties prevent CBD from being treated as a harmless insomnia remedy.

## What the 2026 Cochrane review assessed

The review included randomised studies in adults with insomnia. Eight compared medical cannabis with placebo and one with amitriptyline. Participants were aged 18 to 76 and roughly 73% were women. Six studies involved chronic insomnia and three acute insomnia.

“Medical cannabis” covered different interventions, including plant-based products, synthetic cannabinoids and magistral preparations. The pooled result is therefore not a specific test of every CBD oil sold to consumers.

### Main findings

Compared with placebo, the synthesis indicated:

- little to no clinically meaningful effect on sleep quality or satisfaction;

- an estimated average difference of 7.68 more minutes of sleep, with a 95% confidence interval ranging from about 23 fewer to 38 more minutes: this does not establish a reliable benefit;

- little to no effect on daytime functioning;

- a possible slight increase in adverse events, supported by low-certainty evidence.

Certainty for the sleep outcomes was very low. Better-designed trials could materially change these estimates.

### Why this is not a final answer about CBD

Meta-analysis cannot remove fundamental differences between studies. Product composition, dose, treatment duration, type of insomnia and measurement methods varied. Pooling medical cannabis, synthetic cannabinoids and preparations with different THC-to-CBD ratios is not equivalent to testing one standardised CBD product.

## What the 150 mg isolated-CBD trial found

A 2024 randomised pilot trial compared 150 mg of sublingual CBD each night with placebo in 30 people with moderate-to-severe primary insomnia. After two weeks, insomnia severity, self-reported sleep-onset latency, self-reported sleep efficiency and wake after sleep onset did not differ between groups.

The CBD group showed some favourable signals, including better objective sleep efficiency after two weeks and higher well-being scores. But the sample was very small, treatment was brief and most outcomes were no different from placebo. The authors concluded that further controlled trials across doses and treatment periods were needed.

The figure “150 mg” describes the study protocol. It is not a recommended dose and should not be directly compared with a retail product.

## Isolated CBD, THC and mixtures are not interchangeable

Using “cannabinoids” as if it meant CBD creates confusion. THC can affect sleepiness, perception and sleep architecture, but it also has psychoactive effects and different risks. A full-spectrum extract is not equivalent to purified CBD. Two oils with the same stated concentration may also differ in composition, contaminants and label accuracy.

To interpret a study, ask:

- which substance was tested;

- the amount and route of administration;

- treatment duration;

- the population studied;

- subjective and objective outcomes;

- the comparator.

## Why personal experience is not enough

Sleep is sensitive to expectations, routines, stress, environment and natural symptom fluctuation. Feeling more relaxed does not necessarily mean sleeping longer or more efficiently. Sleep diaries, questionnaires and actigraphy can produce different results.

A testimonial describes an individual experience; it cannot establish efficacy, safety or causation. The same limitation applies to uncontrolled case series.

## Safety and interactions cannot be separated from efficacy

CBD not producing the same intoxicating effect as THC does not make it biologically inactive. The FDA warns about possible liver injury, drug interactions and changes in alertness, including drowsiness. Combining CBD with alcohol or medicines that slow brain activity may increase sedation and injury risk.

In February 2026, EFSA published a provisional safe intake level for CBD as a novel food of 0.0275 mg per kilogram of body weight per day—about 2 mg per day for a 70 kg adult. This is NOT a general threshold for all products: it applies only to certain food supplements with at least 98% pure CBD, no nanoparticles, an appropriately assessed production process and genotoxicity ruled out. EFSA could not establish CBD safety for people under 25, those who are pregnant or breastfeeding, or those taking medicines. The provisional level is neither an effective dose nor a marketing authorisation or insomnia recommendation. Experimental trial amounts must not be translated into self-directed use.

People taking medicines, living with liver disease, pregnant or breastfeeding, or experiencing daytime drowsiness should not make independent treatment decisions and should seek professional advice.

## CBD does not replace an insomnia assessment

Difficulty falling asleep, frequent waking or unrefreshing sleep can have different causes, including habits, stress, breathing disorders, pain, medicines and physical or mental health conditions. Using a product without assessment can mask the problem or delay appropriate care.

If sleep problems persist, worsen or interfere with daytime activities, discuss them with a healthcare professional. The 2023 European Insomnia Guideline recommends insomnia-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-I) as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults, including delivery by qualified professionals in person or digitally. An appropriate care plan requires individual assessment: this article does not diagnose conditions or prescribe treatment.

## How to read CBD-and-sleep headlines

Check seven questions:

1. Is the study about isolated CBD or a THC-containing product?

2. Is it randomised research or testimonials?

3. How many people were studied, and for how long?

4. Which outcome improved—questionnaire score, actigraphy or total sleep time?

5. Was the effect statistically significant and clinically meaningful?

6. Who funded the study?

7. Is the source discussing efficacy or safety? A safety limit does not prove benefit.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is CBD a sleeping pill?

There is no robust evidence supporting CBD as a proven sleeping medicine. Clinical results are heterogeneous, and the 2026 Cochrane review rates the available evidence as highly uncertain.

### Can CBD improve sleep quality?

Some studies report improvements in selected measures; others find no difference from placebo. Results cannot be generalised across different products, doses or people.

### Is there an established dose for sleep?

No. Research amounts vary and are not instructions for use. Safety, interactions, product quality and regulatory status must be considered separately.

### Is CBD safe because it is natural?

Natural does not mean risk-free. Drowsiness, drug interactions and other adverse effects are possible, while long-term safety and the quality of many non-pharmaceutical products remain uncertain.

## Conclusion

Current evidence does not support a simple promise that CBD improves sleep. The most recent synthesis points to uncertain benefits, small studies and formulations that are difficult to compare. The useful question is not simply “does it work?” but: which substance, in whom, at what amount, for how long and with what level of certainty?
