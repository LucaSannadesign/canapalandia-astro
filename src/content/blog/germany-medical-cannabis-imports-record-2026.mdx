Germany has become Europe’s largest regulated cannabis market. But behind that label, widely repeated by international media, lies a more complex reality than a headline might suggest: growth is being driven primarily by **medical cannabis**, not by a nationwide network of recreational retail stores.

The most striking figure concerns imports. In the first quarter of 2026, Germany imported **67,569 kilograms of cannabis for medical and medical-scientific purposes**. That is a new record, roughly 12% above the previous quarter and about 37% higher than the same period in 2025.

The figure deserves attention for another reason. The official total was initially reported as 50,539 kilograms. Germany’s Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM) later revised it upward by about 34%.

That correction changes the interpretation of the market. There was no quarterly decline of the kind described in several articles published earlier in the year. On the contrary, the first quarter of 2026 was the highest level recorded to date.

## A revision that changes the story

BfArM statistics are updated when delayed reports from market participants are received. The newest figures should therefore be treated as provisional and can rise substantially after their initial publication.

For the first quarter of 2026, the difference is significant:

- initially reported figure: **50,539 kg**;
- revised figure: **67,569 kg**;
- revision: approximately **+33.7%**;
- compared with Q4 2025: approximately **+12%**;
- compared with Q1 2025: approximately **+37%**.

The increase looks even more pronounced when viewed against the period before Germany’s cannabis reform took full effect. In the first quarter of 2024, imports were about 8,143 kilograms. Two years later, quarterly volume was more than eight times higher.

That does not mean that every kilogram imported was immediately sold to patients. The statistics measure cannabis entering Germany’s authorized medical and scientific supply chain. They can include inventory, material intended for processing and quantities that are later re-exported.

## Why Germany’s market grew so quickly

The key change came on **1 April 2024**, when Germany’s cannabis reform entered into force.

On the medical side, cannabis was removed from the general narcotics framework and regulated under the Medizinal-Cannabisgesetz. The change reduced several administrative barriers to prescribing and supported the growth of telemedicine platforms, private prescriptions and specialized pharmacies.

The result was a rapid increase in legal demand and imports. Germany imported about 72.7 tonnes in 2024; in 2025, the total exceeded 200 tonnes. According to a Prohibition Partners estimate cited by the *Financial Times*, Germany’s regulated market was close to one billion dollars in value in 2025.

Domestic production is increasing but remains much smaller. Germany therefore still relies heavily on imports, particularly from Canada, Portugal and Denmark. Country-of-origin data, however, are not yet fully aligned with the latest revision of the quarterly total and should be interpreted cautiously.

## “Regulated market” does not mean unrestricted retail sales

The phrase can be misleading. Since 2024, adults resident in Germany have been allowed to possess limited quantities of cannabis, grow up to three plants for personal use and join non-profit cultivation associations.

The law allows, in particular:

- possession of up to 25 grams in public;
- possession of up to 50 grams at home;
- home cultivation of up to three plants;
- distribution to members through authorized, non-commercial cultivation associations.

Germany does not, however, have a nationwide commercial network of recreational dispensaries or cannabis shops. For-profit recreational sales remain prohibited, while medical cannabis is distributed through pharmacies and requires a prescription.

Germany therefore represents a hybrid model: **rapidly expanding medical access, partial decriminalization of personal use and no generalized commercial recreational market**.

## Telemedicine and online prescriptions at the center of the debate

The sector’s growth has triggered a political dispute. The German government argues that part of the rise in imports has not been matched by a comparable increase in prescriptions reimbursed by the public health insurance system, which have grown much more slowly.

The Federal Ministry of Health has therefore proposed amendments to the Medizinal-Cannabisgesetz. For a first prescription, the proposal would require direct personal contact between doctor and patient and would seek to restrict mail-order delivery of cannabis flower. Follow-up prescriptions could still be issued through telemedicine, provided that an in-person consultation takes place periodically.

The stated aim is to curb platforms that use the medical pathway as an easier route to cannabis for non-therapeutic use. The proposed changes are still being debated and have raised concerns among patients, doctors, pharmacies and industry operators.

The challenge is clear: addressing abuse without recreating the barriers that for years made access difficult for patients with a genuine therapeutic need.

## Consumption, the illicit market and health: the data do not give one simple answer

Germany’s debate remains polarized. Conservative parties describe the reform as a risk to prevention, public safety and youth protection. Supporters of regulation emphasize reduced criminalization of consumers and the possibility of shifting part of demand away from the illicit market.

The scientific EKOCAN evaluation, coordinated by University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf together with the universities of Düsseldorf and Tübingen, has not produced a simple conclusion so far. Initial findings indicate that overall consumption has not increased significantly, while some purchases appear to have shifted toward legal channels, home cultivation and social networks.

Important questions remain open, including THC potency, hospital admissions associated with consumption, prevention among younger people and the still-limited spread of cultivation associations. The final evaluation of the law is expected in 2028.

The German experience therefore shows that regulation does not automatically eliminate every problem. It does, however, make it easier to measure different forms of access separately and to respond with more targeted tools than prohibition alone.

## What it means for Europe and Italy

Germany’s scale is changing the balance of the European market. A country of more than 80 million people is creating pharmaceutical demand large enough to influence production, prices, certification standards and investment beyond its borders.

The German model remains incomplete and sometimes contradictory, but it highlights at least three points that are difficult to ignore:

1. medical cannabis can move from a marginal sector to a structured market when administrative barriers are reduced;
2. decriminalizing possession and home cultivation is not the same as opening an unregulated commercial market;
3. policy adjustments should be based on data rather than on conflating patients, consumers and organized crime.

For Italy, the comparison is unavoidable. While Germany is debating how to regulate a rapidly expanding medical market, many Italian patients continue to report difficulties with access, shortages and regional differences. At the same time, political debate still often blurs the distinctions between medical cannabis, industrial hemp, CBD and recreational use.

Germany’s latest figures do not prove that one model can simply be copied elsewhere. They do show that Europe is already moving and that the legal cannabis market is no longer a marginal hypothesis.

## A European record that needs to be read accurately

Germany is now Europe’s leading regulated cannabis market, driven above all by the expansion of its medical channel. The revised figure of 67.6 tonnes imported in just three months reinforces that position and corrects the earlier impression that growth had stalled at the beginning of 2026.

This is not full commercial legalization, nor is it a system without problems. It is a large-scale regulatory experiment in which therapeutic access, personal freedom, public health and economic interests are still seeking a workable balance.

That is precisely why it is worth examining without slogans: distinguishing data from interpretation and regulation from unrestricted sales.
