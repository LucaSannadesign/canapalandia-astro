*What the EU Court’s 5 November 2026 judgment actually says about CBD foods, organic certification and consumer trust.*

A hemp plant can be grown organically. But that does not mean that every food ingredient made from it is automatically authorised for sale. The distinction sounds straightforward until CBD enters the picture, bringing with it green labels, naturalness claims and a regulatory framework that is often flattened into a slogan.

On 5 November 2026, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled in Case C-495/25 that a CBD-based food lacking the necessary EU marketing authorisation cannot receive organic certification. This is neither a ban on organic hemp cultivation nor a judicial finding that CBD is inherently dangerous. It is a judgment about the limits of what an organic certificate can legitimately say.

## How a French dispute reached Luxembourg

France’s INAO had told organic certification bodies that products based on natural CBD — apart from hemp oil not enriched with CBD — and CBD-enriched foods could not receive organic certification. The Syndicat professionnel du chanvre challenged that position before France’s Conseil d’État, which asked the EU Court to clarify the relationship between organic-production rules and the Novel Food regime.

The Court drew a crucial distinction. A substance may fall within the broad category of processed agricultural products intended as food, and thus within the scope of organic rules, without being eligible for organic certification. Where the product is a Novel Food that has not been authorised for placement on the EU market, the certificate cannot bridge the gap.

## Organic certification is not a marketing licence

EU Regulation 2018/848 addresses organic production and labelling. The Novel Food Regulation 2015/2283 addresses whether certain foods and ingredients may enter the EU market, subject to evidence and authorisation. They answer different questions. Using one in place of the other would be like presenting a vehicle inspection certificate when someone asks for a driving licence: both may matter, but they are not interchangeable.

The judges also recognised the consumer-facing meaning of an organic label. Beyond its technical definition, it conveys a positive impression of quality and approval. If it were placed on a food without the required market authorisation, the label could create unwarranted confidence. A genuine fact about how something is grown should not become an implied endorsement of everything else about it.

## Organic hemp is not the same as CBD-enriched food

The judgment does not prohibit organic hemp cultivation. [Hemp seed oil](/blog/olio-semi-canapa-uso-conservazione-cbd/) that has not been enriched with CBD was expressly treated separately in the French dispute. By contrast, adding CBD to a food can raise Novel Food authorisation requirements that cannot be erased by calling the ingredients natural or by placing a leaf on the packaging.

This distinction is essential for responsible communication. A food-grade hemp seed oil and an edible CBD-enriched oil can look similar, but their ingredients and regulatory paths are not necessarily the same. The label must not do more work than the law allows it to do.

## What the judgment does — and does not — settle

CBD intended for ingestion is addressed through the EU Novel Food framework. An EFSA scientific assessment is not itself a market authorisation, and a favourable conclusion for one precisely defined dossier does not authorise every other extract or formulation. The Court did not decide individual CBD applications or declare a universal new sales ban across every CBD product category.

It did, however, clarify a concrete consequence for an unauthorised food: EU organic certification cannot be used as a reputational shortcut. The interpretation also applies when that product is intended for export to a non-EU country, because confidence in the EU certification scheme matters beyond the Union’s borders. This does not replace the laws of the destination country.

## What producers and buyers should take away

For businesses, the logical order is: identify the precise product and intended use, establish its food-law status, and only then assess the conditions for organic certification. A batch certificate of analysis can describe composition, but it cannot substitute for either a Novel Food authorisation or an organic certificate.

For buyers, a useful habit is to read ingredients, intended use, regulatory status and organic claims separately. Products that share a hemp leaf on their packaging need not share the same legal status.

The Court’s answer is a preliminary ruling that binds the interpretation of EU law; the French court must still resolve the national dispute. It should not be recast as a universal seizure of products or a scientific verdict on CBD toxicity.

## The larger point

The judgment insists on consistency between food authorisation and organic certification. That is reasonable. Yet it also exposes a sector-wide communication problem: hemp, CBD, natural and organic are often treated as interchangeable words when they describe very different things.

The useful conclusion is not that Europe has banned organic hemp, nor that organic farming opens every regulatory door. It is that a credible label must mean something specific. If it turns into a shortcut around other safeguards, it serves neither serious producers nor consumers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Has the EU Court banned organic hemp?

No. The ruling concerns the organic certification of CBD-based food products that require, but lack, authorisation under the Novel Food rules. It does not ban organic cultivation of hemp.

### Is organic hemp seed oil the same as CBD-enriched edible oil?

No. Hemp seed oil that has not been enriched with CBD is a different product from a food to which cannabidiol has been added. Each product must be assessed by its actual ingredients and intended use.

### Does an organic label automatically authorise a product as food?

No. Organic production rules and food-market authorisation are separate requirements, both of which must be satisfied where applicable.

### Does the judgment declare CBD toxic?

No. The ruling addresses eligibility for organic certification in the absence of the required food-market authorisation. It is not a general toxicity assessment.
