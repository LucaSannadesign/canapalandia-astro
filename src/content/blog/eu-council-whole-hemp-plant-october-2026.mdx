On 19 October 2026, the Council of the European Union agreed a negotiating position on a revision of the EU's agricultural market rules. The industrial hemp sector is part of that decision: under the approach supported by the Council, the sector would cover all parts of the hemp plant. This is a substantive political and legislative development, but it does not mean hemp flowers can now be freely sold in Italy.

## What happened on 19 October 2026?

The Council adopted a partial negotiating mandate to update the common organisation of agricultural markets (CMO) as part of the EU's 2028–2034 budget framework. The CMO establishes common rules governing agricultural markets across the Union.

In its dedicated hemp section, the official Council press release says the institution retained the Commission's proposal to harmonise production and marketing rules across countries. The proposed sector would encompass all parts of the hemp plant, rather than fibre and seeds alone.

The timeline matters. A mandate to negotiate was approved on 19 October; the release was updated with the partial negotiating text on 20 October. Neither step amounts to the final adoption of a regulation that already applies.

## Why does “the whole plant” matter?

A hemp crop produces stalks, fibres, woody core, seeds and other plant material. Recognising the full crop within the agricultural market framework could make rules for handling these fractions more coherent and improve predictability across cross-border supply chains.

That recognition would not automatically authorise every possible commercial use. Finished goods may also be governed by food safety, medicines, cosmetics, narcotics, public-health and national rules. The distinction between an agricultural raw material and a consumer product remains essential.

## What does the 0.3% THC threshold mean?

The Council refers to strict seed certification and a 0.3% Δ9-THC threshold in the proposed agricultural framework. It would be misleading to present that figure as a universal marketing threshold automatically making every flower, extract or cannabinoid product legal.

Conformity depends on the context of cultivation, applicable testing rules and the legal category of the finished product. A threshold linked to agricultural rules does not replace other product-specific or national requirements.

## What changes in Italy today?

Not much in immediate legal terms. The Council's negotiating position does not itself repeal or suspend Italian provisions currently in force. In particular, it does not automatically remove restrictions introduced in 2025 on inflorescences and certain products derived from them. Those rules still need to be considered alongside ongoing court proceedings and applicable EU law.

For growers and processors, this is therefore a development to watch, not an operational authorisation. Businesses should not enter transactions or advertise products on the assumption that a general liberalisation has already been enacted.

## Safeguards and national policy space

The official release emphasises public-health protection, strict seed certification and safeguards designed to prevent the future provisions from interfering with national policy in areas such as public security, drugs and health.

This is a reason not to overstate the whole-plant recognition. Even if a final act follows, its actual reach will depend on the agreed wording, implementation and interaction with other applicable laws. A short excerpt of the press release cannot settle how every finished product may be marketed.

## What happens next?

The Council presidency will use the mandate to negotiate with the European Parliament. Only a completed legislative process and a final text can establish what rules will enter into force, on what date and subject to which conditions. Parts of the wider 2028–2034 budget framework also remain to be agreed.

Three milestones matter: a negotiated text between Council and Parliament, the final scope of its hemp provisions, and the timetable for their application. Italian readers will then need to compare those rules with the domestic legislation in force at that moment.

## Three mistakes to avoid

First, calling this a Europe-wide legalisation of “cannabis light”. The Council has not passed a final law or established blanket legal access to consumer products.

Second, treating 0.3% THC as a universal limit for every finished product. Agricultural rules and product categories must be kept separate.

Third, declaring that Italy's restrictions on hemp flowers have already ceased to apply. A European negotiating mandate does not have that effect by itself.

## What the decision means for the hemp sector

The 19 October position matters because it puts the full scope of the agricultural hemp crop on the EU negotiating table. More coordinated rules could help the supply chain over time. For growers, manufacturers and retailers, however, commercial decisions still require a careful reading of rules actually in force.
