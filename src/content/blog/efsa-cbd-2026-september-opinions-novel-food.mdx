Three new EFSA opinions on CBD, published in September 2026, tell a much more nuanced story than a simple “yes” or “no”. One synthetic CBD dossier received a favourable conclusion under the proposed conditions, while safety could not be established for two plant-derived dossiers.

That is an important development, but **it is not a general green light for CBD in food**.

The key point is this: EFSA assesses specific products, with defined compositions, manufacturing processes and conditions of use. The conclusions do not automatically extend to every oil, extract, supplement or food containing CBD. Most importantly, **an EFSA scientific opinion is not yet an authorisation to sell a product in the European Union**.

## From February to September 2026: what changed

On 9 February 2026, EFSA updated its general assessment of cannabidiol as a Novel Food. The Panel derived a **provisional safe level of 0.0275 mg/kg body weight per day**, about **2 mg per day for a 70 kg adult**, applicable only to food-supplement formulations containing at least 98% CBD, without nanoparticles, where the production process is considered safe and genotoxicity has been ruled out.

That provisional level did not automatically apply to people under 25, pregnant or breastfeeding women, or people taking medication.

In September, EFSA moved from that general framework to three product-specific opinions. This is where the picture becomes more concrete.

## Three dossiers, three different outcomes

The three opinions are not interchangeable. The origin of the CBD, its purity, manufacturing process, specifications and proposed conditions of use differ — and each of these elements matters in EFSA’s safety assessment.

### The synthetic CBD dossier

For one specific **synthetic CBD Novel Food with a purity of at least 98%**, intended to be marketed in MCT oil as a food supplement, EFSA concluded that the product could be considered **safe at up to 2 mg per day** under the proposed conditions of use.

The conclusion applies to people aged 25 and over and does not apply during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Another important limitation is that, based on the available data, EFSA **could not establish safety for people taking medication**. This does not mean that every combination of CBD and medicines necessarily causes harm; it means the favourable conclusion cannot automatically be extended to this population.

### CBD isolated from Cannabis sativa L.

A second opinion published on 21 September concerns CBD isolated from *Cannabis sativa L.*, with a purity of at least 98%, proposed as an ingredient in food supplements at up to 60 mg per day.

During the assessment, EFSA requested additional information on identity, production, composition, specifications, genotoxicity, reproductive and developmental toxicity and human data. According to the opinion, the applicant did not provide the requested information.

As a result, **the safety of the product could not be established**.

### Cannabis sativa L. extract

The third opinion concerns a *Cannabis sativa L.* extract containing around 57–65% CBD and 9–17% other cannabinoids, proposed for use in food supplements at up to 30 mg per day.

Again, EFSA identified information gaps and requested additional data. Without the necessary replies, **the safety of the Novel Food could not be established**.

The conclusion is therefore not that “plant-derived CBD is dangerous”. It is more precise: **for those two dossiers and those proposed conditions of use, the available evidence was not sufficient for a positive safety conclusion**.

## What has actually changed

Five practical points emerge from the new opinions:

- one specific synthetic CBD product received a favourable assessment under restricted conditions of use;
- **2 mg per day is not a recommended dose** and does not become a universal limit for all CBD products;
- the distinction between synthetic and plant origin is not enough on its own: what matters is the specific dossier, including specifications and conditions of use;
- safety was not established for the two plant-derived products assessed in September;
- none of these opinions, by itself, authorises the general marketing of CBD across the EU.

## EFSA opinion vs EU authorisation: why they are not the same thing

EFSA and the European Commission have different roles.

EFSA assesses the **scientific safety** of the product covered by a dossier. The Commission, together with the Member States, manages the authorisation stage under the Novel Food Regulation.

Only an authorising act that updates the **Union list of authorised Novel Foods** can determine whether a specific product may be placed on the EU market and under what conditions.

As of 7 October 2026, the Commission’s page listing updates to the Union list does not show a general CBD authorisation resulting from the September opinions.

A favourable EFSA opinion should therefore not be presented as a general authorisation and does not, by itself, justify health or therapeutic claims about CBD.

## What this means for businesses selling or communicating CBD products

For businesses writing product pages, articles or campaigns, the main requirement is **precision**.

Saying that CBD in food is “always prohibited” is too broad. Saying that it is “now authorised” is equally misleading.

Accurate communication should specify:

- which product was assessed;
- the stage reached in the procedure;
- the conditions attached to the dossier;
- which populations are included or excluded from the assessment;
- the difference between EFSA’s scientific assessment and EU authorisation.

A conclusion concerning one specific synthetic CBD product cannot simply be transferred by analogy to plant extracts with a different composition.

## What still needs to be verified

The next developments to watch are:

1. any European Commission decision concerning the favourably assessed synthetic CBD dossier;
2. the final specifications and conditions of use in any authorising act;
3. the progress of the plant-derived dossiers if further data are submitted;
4. the practical impact on labelling, product categories and enforcement in individual Member States.

## In summary

If there is one point to remember, it is this: **the September opinions do not liberalise CBD**, but they show that different dossiers can reach different outcomes depending on the available evidence and the characteristics of the product.

EFSA considered the evidence sufficient for one specific synthetic CBD product under restricted conditions, but could not do the same for two plant-derived products. Under the Novel Food system, “CBD” is not assessed in the abstract; a defined product, with a defined composition, process and use, is assessed.

Until the Commission adopts any authorising act, **there is no general commercial green light**.
