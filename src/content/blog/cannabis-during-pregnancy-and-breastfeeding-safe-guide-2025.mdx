> **In brief:** major health authorities recommend avoiding cannabis, THC and CBD during pregnancy. Stopping cannabis use is also recommended during breastfeeding. If cannabis is being used for nausea, pain, anxiety or another condition, it is important to discuss it with a doctor or gynecologist so that alternatives with a better-known safety profile can be considered.

## Why this article was updated

Information about cannabis, pregnancy and breastfeeding requires particular caution. There is currently no scientific basis for defining any amount or method of cannabis or CBD use as **safe during pregnancy**. For this reason, we updated this guide to remove any suggestion of a possible “safe use” and to distinguish between observed data, limitations in current knowledge, and clinical recommendations.

## Cannabis during pregnancy: what we know

**THC** crosses the placenta and can reach the fetus. Available studies do not always make it possible to completely separate the effects of cannabis from other factors—for example tobacco use, socioeconomic conditions, or use of other substances—but cannabis exposure during pregnancy has been associated with adverse outcomes such as **low birth weight** and **preterm birth**. Possible effects on neurological development remain an area of ongoing research.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), in its Clinical Consensus published in 2025, recommends that healthcare professionals explain perinatal and neonatal risks and **advise patients to stop using cannabis during pregnancy**. The document also states that there are no medical indications for cannabis use during pregnancy.

## What about CBD?

The fact that **CBD does not produce the same psychoactive effect as THC does not mean that it has been shown to be safe during pregnancy**.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) states that there are no comprehensive studies on the effects of CBD on the fetus, the pregnant person, or the breastfed infant. It also highlights possible concerns involving contaminants, undeclared THC, drug interactions, and other adverse effects of CBD. For these reasons, the FDA recommends avoiding CBD, THC and cannabis during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

- <a href="https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/what-you-should-know-about-using-cannabis-including-cbd-when-pregnant-or-breastfeeding" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">FDA — Cannabis, CBD, pregnancy and breastfeeding</a>

This also applies to oils, edibles, vaporizers, infusions and other derived products: **the form of administration does not automatically make cannabinoid exposure safe**.

## Cannabis and breastfeeding

THC is fat-soluble and can pass into breast milk. Data on the short- and long-term effects of exposure through breast milk are still limited, but uncertainty does not mean safety.

ACOG recommends stopping cannabis use during breastfeeding as well. At the same time, it adds an important clarification: if a person continues to use cannabis, that fact alone **should not automatically lead to discouraging or stopping breastfeeding**. The situation should be discussed with healthcare professionals, considering risks, benefits and the possibility of stopping cannabis use in a nonjudgmental way.

The FDA takes a more precautionary position and recommends avoiding cannabis, THC and CBD during breastfeeding too.

## The situation in Italy

Italy has a regulated system for the medical use of cannabis, but this does not mean cannabis is indicated during pregnancy. The Italian Ministry of Health describes the conditions and procedures governing medical cannabis preparations; pregnancy still requires an individual clinical assessment.

In a 2025 update on cannabis and female fertility, the Italian National Institute of Health (Istituto Superiore di Sanità) refers to international recommendations to stop cannabis use during the preconception period and throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding.

## If cannabis is being used for nausea, pain, anxiety or insomnia

Some people use cannabis or CBD to manage pre-existing symptoms or symptoms related to pregnancy. The recommendation is not simply “stop and deal with it”: it is useful to tell your doctor **which symptom it is being used for**, how often, and in what form, so that alternatives with a better-established safety profile during pregnancy can be considered.

Do not stop or replace a prescribed treatment on your own. If medical cannabis is involved, if CBD is taken together with other medications, or if the underlying condition requires treatment, the decision should be discussed with the doctor managing the pregnancy and with the relevant specialist.

## What to avoid

- Do not assume CBD is “safe” simply because it is not intoxicating.
- Do not rely on dosages found online to establish a safe amount during pregnancy: no safety threshold has been defined.
- Do not use products that lack traceability or a verifiable composition.
- Do not replace prescribed therapies with cannabis or CBD without medical assessment.
- Avoid exposing a newborn to cannabis smoke or aerosol.

## Conclusion

Based on the available evidence, **it is not possible to identify a safe use of cannabis, THC or CBD during pregnancy**. Current health recommendations advise stopping use during pregnancy and avoiding it during breastfeeding as well, while maintaining an individualized and non-stigmatizing clinical approach.

The goal is not to moralize cannabis use, but to apply the precautionary principle during a period in which even relatively small exposures may affect two people and in which many aspects of cannabinoids' effects on development remain uncertain.
