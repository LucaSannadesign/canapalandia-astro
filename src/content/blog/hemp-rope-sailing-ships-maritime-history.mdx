A sailor handles one line while another supports the mast and a third holds the vessel at the quay. Before engines, a sailing ship depended on this network of fibres. Much of its cordage was made from hemp: an agricultural material turned into maritime technology.

Following a rope takes us beyond the harbour, through fields, retting ponds, workshops and stores. Venice, the Bologna countryside and Chatham offer three documented perspectives on this supply chain; they are not successive stops on the proven journey of one particular rope.

## Rope was part of the ship, not an accessory

Standing rigging supported the masts, while running rigging helped sailors control the sails and other equipment. Mooring lines and other service ropes had different jobs. Materials, dimensions and treatments varied with function, place and period.

The National Maritime Museum in Greenwich holds a contemporary 1:24 model of the cargo schooner *Victoria* (1838), [accession SLR0766](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-66727), with hemp standing and running rigging. Its sails are modern replacements. The catalogue describes the model rather than an analysis of the original ship's ropes, a crucial distinction when interpreting museum evidence.

Another object, [REL0445](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-63551), is a fragment of tarred hemp rope associated with HMS *Victory* and catalogued as relieving tackle. The catalogue gives a diameter of 28 mm and an overall measurement of 146 mm. This is specific material evidence, not proof that all ropes aboard the ship were made alike.

## Venice and the shipyard's appetite for hemp

In Venice, cordage was an essential part of shipyard organisation. The Corderie of the Arsenale are now celebrated as architectural and cultural spaces, but their original purpose belonged to the everyday logistics of merchant and naval fleets.

In an [account dated 15 January 2019](https://live.comune.venezia.it/index.php/it/2025/04/una-calle-una-storia-cordami-e-corderie) (in Italian), the City of Venice records the Great Council’s decision in 1302 to concentrate hemp storage and rope spinning in a dedicated building, erected in 1303. Its successor, the 316-metre *Teza longa de la Tana*, was designed by Antonio da Ponte and built between 1579 and 1585. Its dimensions reflect the scale of the work, while the names *conzacanevi* and *filacanevi* preserve the memory of specialist hemp workers.

The City of Venice tentatively connects the name Tana with the Don and trading posts near the Sea of Azov that supplied hemp for rope and caulking. Its [account of the pegoloti](https://live.comune.venezia.it/it/node/125835) (in Italian) also describes nearby pitch-working facilities, where long ropes were dipped in heated pitch. Manufacture, protection and logistics were distinct but closely connected activities.

## The farmland behind the fleet

Focusing only on the ship misses the other half of this history. The [Rural Civilization Museum near Bologna](https://www.museociviltacontadina.bo.it/Il_Museo/La_Canapa_una_fibra_versatile) (source in Italian) documents hemp cultivation expanding across the Bologna area from the second half of the fifteenth century, supported in part by demand from the Venetian naval ropeworks.

Farmers and craftspeople supplied more than ships: hemp also went into fishing nets, textiles and other household and industrial products. From the seventeenth century, shipyards in north-western Europe became additional markets. A rope in a harbour therefore connected seafaring to the work of inland communities.

Retting helped separate the fibres from surrounding plant tissues; preparation and combing preceded spinning. The museum also distinguishes [fibre dressing, spinning, weaving and rope making](https://www.museociviltacontadina.bo.it/La_Canapa_una_fibra_versatile/La_lavorazione_della_canapa) (in Italian) as different trades, distributed across farms, workshops and manufacturing premises. The harbour depended on work done far from the sea.

## From prepared fibre to working rope

A ropeworks converted prepared fibres into yarn, yarn into strands, and strands into rope. The sequence sounds straightforward, but achieving consistent twist, strength and dimensions required specialised knowledge.

The [trust responsible for Chatham Historic Dockyard](https://thedockyard.co.uk/master-ropemakers/about-us/our-machinery/) describes a ropewalk some 1,100 feet (335 metres) long. Fibres were combed before spinning; forming machines combined yarn into strands, and closing machines laid strands into finished rope. The trust reports that a forming machine made by Henry Maudslay in 1811 is still in use.

The length of a ropewalk had a purpose: ships needed far more than short cords. Producing long ropes and cables required room and careful handling. Machines mattered, but so did the skills of workers who recognised faults, controlled tension and understood the materials.

## Why hemp, and why not for everything?

Hemp supplied long fibres well suited to rope production. That did not make natural cordage immune to moisture, wear or demanding loads. Tar treatments were used in some maritime applications, while rope choice depended on its job and the conditions it would face.

Nor should we assume that every historic sail was hemp, or that modern ropes can all be replaced without trade-offs. Linen and other plant fibres also played roles, while synthetic materials later reshaped the industry. The historical question is why hemp mattered in a particular system, not whether it was universally superior.

## A craft preserved in museums and city spaces

Venice's Corderie remain part of a remarkable architectural landscape; Chatham still tells the story of rope manufacture through working spaces and equipment. Museum collections—from a small rope fragment to a detailed rigged ship model—give physical shape to an economy that is otherwise easy to overlook.

Perhaps the most compelling part of the story is how ordinary its key object seems. A rope that helped a vessel sail might begin as a plant growing in a field, travel through warehouses and workshops, and pass through numerous hands before reaching the deck.

That is a better reason to remember hemp than any grand myth about a forgotten miracle crop: the history is already visible in shipyards, archives and the ropes that survived.
