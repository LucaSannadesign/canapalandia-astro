Hemp has a respectable résumé: fibre, seeds, building materials. According to some of its most enthusiastic publicity, it has another superpower: growing almost without water. Unfortunately, roots don't read press releases.

So how much water does industrial hemp actually use? The short answer is: it depends. Not as an escape hatch, but because the conditions and the way water is measured change the answer.

## Numbers first, slogans later

A scientific review first published online in December 2025, and included in the 2026 volume of the Journal of Cannabis Research, brought together the available evidence. Reported seasonal water-use figures ranged roughly from 220 to 700 mm, with values around 4–5 mm per day frequently observed [[1]](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42238-025-00370-z).

These are not universal irrigation instructions. They come from different regions, seasons, crop types and methods. A 2026 paper drawing on experiments in Kansas from 2019 to 2022 reported seasonal water use of 250–716 mm [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1002/agj2.70331). The range is part of the story, not a flaw to hide.

If a promotional claim offers one precise number without saying where and how it was measured, key information is missing.

## Millions of litres? Yes. But which water?

Here is a simple unit conversion, not a recommended irrigation schedule.

One millimetre over one square metre equals one litre. Across one hectare, one millimetre corresponds to 10,000 litres. A hypothetical seasonal depth of 300 mm amounts to 3 million litres per hectare when expressed as a volume over that area.

That does NOT mean a grower must pump 3 million litres onto a hemp field.

Water used by crops can be supplied by rainfall and stored soil moisture as well as irrigation. And different studies report evapotranspiration, applied irrigation or water-footprint indicators. They are not interchangeable.

Evapotranspiration describes water moving from soil and plants into the atmosphere. Irrigation is water intentionally added to the field. A water footprint uses a defined accounting method and may distinguish different components and products.

Rain doesn't arrive with a utility bill. It still matters to the crop's water balance.

## Drought tolerance is not the same as unchanged yield

Hemp can develop roots that explore deeper soil layers and can respond physiologically when water becomes scarce. That does not turn it into a drought-proof crop.

A July 2026 review in Frontiers in Plant Science describes how water stress can affect growth, yield and plant physiology [[3]](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2026.1890092). Surviving a dry spell and producing the same amount of usable fibre or grain are two different achievements.

Timing matters, too. Establishment and phases of rapid growth may respond differently to water shortage. Calling a crop 'resilient' without mentioning output is like celebrating a car that keeps moving on an almost empty tank without asking how far or how fast it can go.

## Mediterranean growing conditions are their own question

A 2026 experimental study of the hemp variety Felina 32 explored how soil hydraulic properties affect irrigation decisions in a Mediterranean setting [[4]](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy16060649). Its takeaway is not a fixed amount of irrigation water for every grower. It is that soil texture and the way soil stores and releases moisture influence management.

A field with reliable rainfall is not equivalent to one facing long dry periods. Nor do two soils with very different water-holding properties behave alike.

For Mediterranean producers, the important question is not whether hemp has a green reputation. It is whether the local climate, soil and available resources can support a viable crop without worsening water pressure.

## Does hemp use less water than cotton?

There is evidence behind the comparison. A 2023 analysis drawing on 28 published sources estimated a 38% lower crop water requirement and a 60% lower water footprint for hemp compared with cotton, using its specified datasets and definitions [[5]](https://doi.org/10.54386/jam.v25i3.2260).

Those percentages are useful research findings, not universal labels that can be attached to every hemp T-shirt.

Region, yield, irrigation systems, product and system boundaries all matter. Producing a fibre crop is not identical to processing, dyeing, transporting and manufacturing finished fabric.

In some contexts, hemp offers a meaningful advantage. That is a stronger claim than pretending one percentage settles every environmental comparison.

## Five questions worth asking

Before describing hemp as a low-water crop:

1. Where, and in which growing season, was it cultivated?

2. Does the number describe rainfall, irrigation, evapotranspiration or a water footprint?

3. Was the crop grown for fibre, grain or something else?

4. How much usable output was obtained per hectare?

5. Does the comparison stop at the farm gate or include processing?

Without those answers, we may be reading a marketing slogan, not an environmental assessment.

## Canapalandia's view: fewer miracles, better measurements

Industrial hemp has promising applications and may fit water-efficient farming systems in suitable conditions. That is worth discussing without inflating it into a miracle.

Serious support for a crop means examining the evidence — including the inconvenient details about yield, location and water availability.

Hemp isn't a cactus. It doesn't need to be one to deserve a closer look.
