Long before hemp became the subject of legislative controversy, it was an everyday raw material. People grew it for fibre and seed, used it in textiles, nets and ropes, and built agricultural and manufacturing trades around it. In other settings the same botanical species acquired ritual and medicinal meanings. There is no single history of hemp, but a series of connected histories shaped by places, techniques and social choices.

Understanding them means distinguishing physical evidence from interpretation, documented events from legends, and contemporary industrial opportunities from unsupported promises.

## Origins: what the evidence actually tells us

A 2021 population-genetics study by Guangpeng Ren and colleagues in *Science Advances*, based on the sequencing of Cannabis sativa genomes, proposed an initial domestication in East Asia during the Neolithic. Its model places the divergence of the ancestral lineage from domesticated populations about 12,000 years ago. This is a genetic inference, not a direct dating of a particular textile or cultivated field. [Ren et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284894/).

Domestication was a process rather than a single event. Over time, human selection favoured different characteristics, including fibre, seed and compounds found in the flowering parts of the plant. Modern regulatory definitions of industrial hemp should therefore not be projected backwards onto ancient societies.

## Antiquity: fibres, written accounts and ritual practices

In the fifth century BCE, Herodotus described hemp in the *Histories* (IV, 74–75), connecting it with Thracian textiles and Scythian purification practices. His account is an important literary source, but it is not a comprehensive inventory of ancient uses. [Herodotus, book IV](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Herodotus/4C%2A.html).

A different kind of evidence comes from Jirzankal Cemetery in the eastern Pamirs. A 2019 archaeochemical study identified cannabis residues in funerary braziers dating back about 2,500 years. The context suggests ritual use, but does not establish that all ancient cultivation had the same purpose. [Ren et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561734/).

These examples illustrate why textual accounts, archaeological finds and genetic reconstructions must be distinguished. Evidence of the plant's presence alone does not prove that a particular textile, sail or remedy contained hemp. Such claims require material analysis or specific documentation.

## Medieval and early modern Europe: a crop becomes a supply chain

In preindustrial economies, fibre connected farms and towns. Hemp could become cloth, sacks, nets or rope, but a considerable amount of work separated the harvested crop from the finished object: retting, fibre separation, preparation, spinning, and finally weaving or rope making.

Naval arsenals offer a particularly clear example of demand for cordage and the need for dependable supplies. Venice developed specialised facilities, while the Bologna countryside supplied fibre to distant markets. Explore the techniques and museum evidence in [When Hemp Held Sailing Ships Together](/blog/hemp-rope-sailing-ships-maritime-history/).

Hemp was also part of the history of papermaking, often through the use of textile rags. However, not all historic paper was made from hemp; its composition depended on available raw materials and local processes.

## Italy: fields, retting ponds and skilled labour

The Rural Civilisation Museum at Bentivoglio traces the expansion of hemp cultivation around Bologna, Budrio and Cento from the second half of the fifteenth century. In subsequent centuries it became characteristic of extensive areas of the Emilia-Romagna plain, supporting farmers, merchants and manufacturers.

Vincenzo Tanara's agricultural treatise *Economia del cittadino in villa*, published in 1664, provides a historical account of the demanding work involved in fibre processing. The museum preserves this heritage through tools, photographs and working environments. [Museum account of hemp cultivation and domestic industry](https://www.museociviltacontadina.bo.it/Guida/La_coltivazione_e_lindustria_domestica_della_canapa) (Italian).

Italy's hemp history was more than the story of a profitable crop. It involved regional specialisation, household labour and the relationship between farming and manufacturing. When the system declined during the twentieth century, skills, markets and infrastructure were also lost. [Museum account of the industry's decline](https://www.museociviltacontadina.bo.it/Home_test/Home_test/esplora_sezioni_museo/La_Canapa_una_fibra_versatile) (Italian).

## Across the Atlantic: documents and legends

In Britain's North American colonies, hemp became an agricultural fibre crop. George Washington's letter to James Gildart dated 20 September 1765 records his interest in prices, shipping and potential returns from hemp and flax. It is evidence about farming and trade, not about psychoactive consumption. [Washington's letter in the Mount Vernon collection](https://www.mountvernon.org/education/primary-source-collections/primary-source-collections/article/george-washington-letter-to-james-gildart).

A familiar claim concerns the American Declaration of Independence. The National Archives confirms that the official signed 1776 document was engrossed on parchment, not hemp paper. Early printed copies, such as the Dunlap Broadside, are separate documents; their paper composition cannot be inferred from the signed version. [National Archives: Declaration of Independence](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/declaration-of-independence).

## The twentieth century: why the industry declined

The decline cannot be explained by a single law or industry. In the United States the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937 imposed federal requirements that discouraged production. During the Second World War, Hemp for Victory promoted renewed cultivation for military supplies. Restrictions returned after the war, and the Controlled Substances Act of 1970 reorganised the federal framework.

The US Department of Agriculture describes these developments in its 2019 documentation on the domestic hemp programme. [Federal Register, 31 October 2019](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2019-10-31/html/2019-23749.htm).

In Italy and elsewhere, changes in agriculture, competing materials and the evolution of textile manufacturing also contributed to the decline. Legal restrictions and the stigma surrounding cannabis mattered, but they do not erase the other causes. A single conspiracy narrative cannot explain the conditions under which an entire supply chain disappeared.

## Rediscovery and contemporary hemp

Over recent decades, hemp has returned to agricultural and industrial programmes. Italy's Law 242 of 2016 marked a step in the promotion of the agroindustrial supply chain, while the US Farm Bills of 2014 and 2018 changed the federal framework. These are historical milestones, not a summary of the rules currently applicable to every hemp product. [Italian Law 242/2016](https://www.normattiva.it/eli/id/2016/12/30/16G00258/ORIGINAL) (Italian) and [USDA documentation](https://www.ams.usda.gov/rules-regulations/hemp/HempLawsandRegulations).

Contemporary applications include textiles, composites, construction materials and seed-based products. The [European Commission](https://agriculture.ec.europa.eu/farming/crops/hemp_en) outlines opportunities for the crop in agriculture and industry. Environmental performance nevertheless depends on farming and processing conditions, product durability and the alternatives being compared. Hemp cannot be assumed to require less water or have a lower environmental footprint in every situation.

For the evidence and limitations behind such comparisons, see [How much water does hemp use?](/blog/how-much-water-does-industrial-hemp-use/).

## Revisiting the past without mythology

Hemp's historical significance lies in the relationships between people, materials and technology. Archaeology documents particular ancient practices; agricultural and commercial sources reveal complex economies; twentieth-century legislation shows how political choices can shape crops and industries.

Rediscovering this history means recovering the visibility of workers and supply chains, rather than turning a plant into a symbol without contradictions. That concrete knowledge provides a better starting point for assessing hemp's opportunities today.
