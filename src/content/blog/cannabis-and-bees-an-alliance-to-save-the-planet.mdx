A bee on a hemp plant can suggest a simple story: hemp attracts pollinators, so it must be “good for bees”. The reality is more interesting — and more precise.

*Cannabis sativa* is mainly wind-pollinated. It does not produce nectar and it does not depend on bees in the way many fruit and vegetable crops do. Yet when pollen-producing flowers are present, hemp fields can be visited by honeybees, bumblebees and several wild bee species. [1][2]

The useful question is therefore not whether “hemp and bees can save the planet”, but this: **when, and under which conditions, can hemp provide a useful resource for pollinators?**

## In brief

- Hemp is primarily anemophilous: its pollen is transported mainly by wind.
- It produces no nectar, so bees visit it chiefly to collect pollen.
- Fields with male flowers or other pollen-producing structures can attract many bee species.
- In some North American studies, hemp flowering overlapped with periods when other floral resources were relatively scarce.
- Not all hemp crops provide the same amount of pollen: cultivar, plant sex and production system matter.
- Hemp pollen can contribute to bee nutrition, but it should not be treated as a complete food source on its own.
- The main field evidence cited here comes from the United States and cannot automatically be extrapolated to Italian or European farming landscapes.

## Do bees pollinate hemp?

Not in the same sense that they pollinate apple trees, courgettes or many other insect-dependent plants.

Hemp releases pollen mainly into the air. Male flowers produce large quantities of light pollen grains suited to wind dispersal. When bees enter a hemp field, their presence is therefore best understood primarily as **foraging**: they are collecting a food resource. [4]

That distinction matters because a plant can be visited by many insects without depending on them for reproduction.

Oregon State University also highlights how much the production system matters. In seed production, pollen is necessary and pollen-producing plants or flowers are present. In crops managed to keep female inflorescences unfertilized, pollination is generally avoided. [4]

## If hemp produces no nectar, why do bees visit it?

For pollen.

Hemp does not offer a sugary nectar reward, but pollen-producing flowers can become a foraging resource.

A 2019 study in *Biomass and Bioenergy* examined hemp fields in northern Colorado. Researchers collected bees from 23 genera; honeybees accounted for 38% of the individuals, while wild bees were also well represented. [1]

There is an important detail: in that region, hemp flowered from late July to late September, during a period the authors described as relatively poor in other pollinator-friendly crops. The observed value therefore depended not only on the plant itself, but also on **the timing of flowering and the surrounding agricultural landscape**. [1]

This does not prove that every hemp field, in every climate or region, provides the same benefit.

## What did the Cornell University study observe?

Another study, published in 2020 in *Environmental Entomology* by Cornell University researchers, examined bees visiting hemp fields in New York State. [2]

The researchers identified 16 bee species. More simplified agricultural landscapes were associated with lower wild-bee abundance, while taller hemp cultivars were correlated with greater bee abundance and species richness in the fields studied. [2]

The result reinforces a broader point: **hemp does not act in isolation**. A field within a landscape rich in semi-natural habitats, field margins and flowering plants is not equivalent to a crop surrounded by a highly simplified agricultural environment.

Here too, summer flowering could provide pollen at a time when other resources were less available. That is a potential advantage, but it remains specific to the conditions studied.

## The key limitation: not all hemp fields provide the same resource

Talking simply about “a hemp field” can be misleading.

*Cannabis sativa* includes dioecious and monoecious forms and is grown for different purposes: fibre, seed, biomass or inflorescences. The amount of pollen available can therefore vary dramatically. [3][4]

A crop with a substantial presence of male flowers can offer abundant pollen. A crop made up predominantly of female plants and managed to prevent fertilisation may provide a far more limited pollen resource.

Results from seed or fibre systems should therefore not be transferred automatically to completely different production systems.

## Is hemp pollen nutritious for bees?

It can provide nutrients, but “a lot of pollen” does not automatically mean “a complete diet”.

A 2023 study in *Insects* compared pollen from four industrial-hemp cultivars and visits by different bee groups. The cultivars differed in protein, amino-acid and fatty-acid composition, and the most visited cultivar was not the one with the highest values for every nutrient measured. [5]

The authors concluded that hemp pollen can contribute to bee nutrition, but should not be regarded as sufficient as the sole pollen source for growth and development. [5]

This reflects a broader ecological principle: **diversity of food resources matters more than dependence on a single crop**.

## The evidence is not yet proof for Europe

This is one of the most important limits of the current evidence.

The two most frequently cited field studies on hemp and bee communities were carried out in Colorado and New York State. Climate, flowering calendars, bee communities, farming systems and landscape structure can all differ substantially from European conditions. [1][2]

It is therefore reasonable to say that hemp **can** provide pollen used by bees and that, in certain contexts, it may temporarily add a resource when others are scarce. It is not correct to conclude that growing hemp will automatically increase pollinator biodiversity in Italy or Europe.

That claim would require local, longer-term field studies measuring not only how many bees visit the crop, but also effects on nutrition, reproduction and populations over time.

## A more recent caution: pollen also reflects the growing environment

In 2025, a study in *Chemosphere* added an important caveat in a very specific context: phytoremediation of PFAS-contaminated soil. [6]

In controlled greenhouse experiments, hemp plants grown in contaminated soils accumulated some PFAS in their pollen. The authors therefore highlighted a potential exposure pathway for bees collecting that pollen. [6]

This **does not mean that pollen from ordinary agricultural hemp is contaminated**. The study concerned plants intentionally grown in contaminated soil and shows why hemp used for phytoremediation should not automatically be described as an ecological benefit without trade-offs.

It is a useful example of how strongly context can change the meaning of the same crop.

## Biodiversity: one field is not enough

The presence of bees on hemp does not replace the resources a pollinator community needs across the whole year.

Hedgerows, flower-rich field margins, meadows, spontaneous vegetation, nesting sites and continuity of forage remain essential. Hemp, when it produces pollen, can at most become one part of this wider mosaic.

The same applies to crop protection. The old claim that hemp “does not require pesticides” is too absolute: hemp can be affected by insect pests and other crop-health problems. Because bees may visit flowering hemp, integrated pest-management research stresses the need to consider non-target organisms when planning treatments and timing. [3]

The most realistic ecological role for hemp is therefore not to be a miracle crop, but to function within farming systems designed with greater diversity and ecological care.

## So, does hemp really help bees?

The most accurate answer is: **it can provide an additional pollen resource, but any benefit depends on context**.

Current evidence shows that:

- several bee species visit pollen-producing hemp flowers;
- in some North American agricultural landscapes, flowering can overlap with periods of low availability of other resources;
- cultivars and production systems affect both the amount and quality of available pollen;
- bees still need a diverse pollen diet;
- bee visits to a crop do not, by themselves, prove an improvement in pollinator populations.

Hemp can therefore play an interesting role in a diversified agroecosystem. But turning it into another universal solution would miss the most useful lesson from the research: **cultivar, landscape, timing and management determine the outcome**.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does hemp produce nectar?

No. Studies documenting bee visits to hemp link them mainly to pollen collection.

### Are bees necessary to pollinate hemp?

No. *Cannabis sativa* is primarily wind-pollinated. Bees can transport pollen while collecting it, but hemp does not depend on them in the way many insect-pollinated crops do.

### Can hemp pollen replace other flowering plants?

No. It can add a resource at certain times, but research on its nutritional composition indicates that bees still benefit from multiple pollen sources.

### Can we say that hemp helps bees in Europe?

We can say that bees use hemp pollen and that there is ecological potential. The strongest field evidence cited here comes from North America, however, so effects in European farming systems should not be assumed without local data.
