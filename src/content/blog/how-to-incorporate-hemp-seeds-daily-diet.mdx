**Hemp seeds** are a food ingredient obtained from *Cannabis sativa*, but they should not be confused with CBD products or preparations made from hemp flowers. In the kitchen they are mainly used either whole or hulled, and their mild, slightly nutty flavor makes them easy to add to both sweet and savory dishes without changing the recipe too much.

The useful point is not to present them as a “superfood” or attach therapeutic claims to them. It is more accurate to treat them for what they are: **oil-rich seeds containing a significant amount of unsaturated fats and protein**, to be included within the context of an overall diet.

## Whole or hulled hemp seeds: what really changes

Whole seeds retain their outer shell. Because of that, they are crunchier and generally contain more fiber than hulled seeds. They work well as a topping or in doughs and batters where a crunchy texture is desirable.

Hulled seeds, often sold as *hemp hearts*, are softer and creamier. They are easier to mix into yogurt, porridge, smoothies, sauces, dressings and finished dishes.

Removing the shell also changes the relative balance of fiber, protein and fat in the final product. Nutritional values should therefore be read on the label of the specific package you buy rather than assumed to be the same for every hemp-seed product.

## How much hemp seed to use

There is no official daily dose of hemp seeds that applies to everyone.

A quantity commonly used in nutrition databases as a reference is **30 grams**, roughly three tablespoons of hulled seeds. USDA data for that amount provide about **166 kcal, 9.5 grams of protein and 14.6 grams of fat**.

Those numbers are useful for understanding the food’s nutritional density, not for creating a prescription. In practice, a smaller amount can be used as a topping or a larger amount inside a recipe, depending on the rest of the meal, individual needs and the instructions on the product label.

For that reason, statements such as “two tablespoons a day are good for…” are best avoided: they turn an ordinary food into a health promise that the data do not justify.

## What hemp seeds provide nutritionally

Analyses of food-grade hemp varieties show that composition varies, but in general the seeds contain substantial amounts of **protein and lipids**.

A 2024 review reports, as a broad range, roughly **25–30% protein and 25–35% lipids** in hemp seeds. A study of 29 industrial-hemp varieties also found significant differences among cultivars, with protein and fat contents varying from one variety to another.

The main fatty acids are usually **linoleic acid**, from the omega-6 family, and **alpha-linolenic acid**, from the omega-3 family. Composition studies also report all essential amino acids.

This does not mean hemp seeds automatically replace every other protein source or that a particular fatty-acid ratio produces clinical effects on its own. The most useful reference remains the nutrition label of the actual product.

## How to use them at breakfast

Hulled hemp seeds can be added directly to **yogurt, kefir, porridge or breakfast cereals**. Their soft texture makes them easy to distribute without grinding.

A simple combination is plain yogurt, fresh fruit, oats and hulled hemp seeds. In porridge, they can be stirred in at the end of cooking, especially if you want their flavor to remain more noticeable.

## How to use them in salads, soups and main dishes

Whole seeds work well on salads and vegetables when you want extra crunch, while hulled seeds provide a softer texture. They can also be added to soups and purées just before serving.

On pasta, rice or whole grains, they can be used as a finishing ingredient together with herbs, vegetables, legumes or cheese.

There is no need to build entire recipes “around hemp”: in many cases they can simply be used as you would use other edible seeds.

## Baking and doughs

Hemp seeds can be added to **bread, focaccia, crackers, biscuits and bars**.

Heat inevitably changes some characteristics of fats and aromas, but that does not mean hemp seeds must only be eaten raw.

If the goal is to preserve more of their delicate flavor and untreated lipid fraction, some of the seeds can be added after baking or used as a topping. If texture and crunch are the priority, they can be mixed directly into the dough.

## Smoothies, creams and dressings

Hulled hemp seeds can be blended with water or plant-based drinks to create a creamy base. They can also be used in hummus, pesto-style sauces, savory creams and dressings, where they mainly contribute texture.

The mixture does not always need to be strained; that depends on the recipe and the texture you want.

Fresh preparations made from seeds and water are perishable foods, however, so they should be refrigerated and consumed within a short period, following normal home food-safety practices.

## How to store them after opening

Hemp seeds contain a high proportion of polyunsaturated fats, which are more sensitive to oxidation than more saturated fats. For that reason, **light, heat, air and time** are the main factors to manage.

A practical approach is simple:

- close the package tightly or move the seeds to an airtight container;
- store them away from heat and direct light;
- follow the producer’s storage instructions;
- for hulled seeds, especially after opening and during warm months, consider refrigeration when it is compatible with the label instructions.

It is not useful to give a universal shelf-life claim such as “they last one year”: shelf life depends on packaging, processing, moisture, temperature and conditions after opening. The package date and manufacturer instructions remain the primary reference.

## When it is better not to use them

A clearly rancid smell, unusual taste, moisture, mold or other visible changes are reasons not to consume the product.

As with other seeds and similar foods, anyone with known food allergies or a history of reactions to specific seeds should be cautious when introducing a new food.

## Hemp seeds, THC and CBD are not the same thing

Food-grade hemp seeds **are not CBD products**.

European rules also establish maximum levels for delta-9-THC equivalents in foods made from hemp seeds: **3.0 mg/kg** for seeds and several processed seed products and **7.5 mg/kg** for hemp-seed oil.

This distinction helps avoid two opposite mistakes: presenting hemp seeds as a dietary source of CBD or treating them as though they were equivalent to hemp flowers. They are different product categories with different uses and rules.

## Five simple combinations to try

- **Breakfast:** plain yogurt, pear or berries, oats and hulled hemp seeds.
- **Lunch:** legume salad with tomatoes, herbs and whole or hulled hemp seeds.
- **On a main dish:** pasta with vegetables, extra-virgin olive oil and seeds added at the end.
- **In a spread:** chickpeas, lemon, tahini and a small amount of hulled hemp seeds blended together.
- **For baking:** bread or crackers with whole hemp seeds in the dough and some sprinkled on top.

## In summary

Hemp seeds can be used in the kitchen in much the same way as many other oil-rich seeds: as an ingredient, topping or source of texture.

The difference between whole and hulled seeds matters mainly for fiber, texture and how they are used; quantity should be considered as part of the overall diet, not as a therapeutic dose.

For storage, the key points are a well-closed package, low exposure to light and heat, and following the producer’s instructions. From a regulatory standpoint, it is also useful to remember that **hemp seeds, hemp-seed oil and CBD products are not synonyms**.
