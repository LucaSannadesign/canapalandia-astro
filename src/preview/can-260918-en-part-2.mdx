Common names in *Cannabis sativa* analyses include myrcene, alpha- and beta-pinene, limonene, beta-caryophyllene, linalool and terpinolene. These molecules also occur in many other plant species. Associating a compound with an olfactory note can be useful as a reference, but it does not mean that the final aroma of a plant is determined by that compound alone.

### Common terpenes and olfactory descriptors

| Compound | Class | Commonly reported olfactory descriptors |
|---|---|---|
| Myrcene | Monoterpene | musky, earthy, fruity |
| Limonene | Monoterpene | citrus, lemon/orange |
| α-pinene | Monoterpene | pine, resinous |
| β-pinene | Monoterpene | woody, resinous |
| β-caryophyllene | Sesquiterpene | peppery, spicy, woody |
| Linalool | Monoterpenoid | floral, lavender |
| Terpinolene | Monoterpene | floral, herbal, citrus |

These descriptors are sensory references associated with individual compounds or profiles in which they make a meaningful contribution. By themselves they do not predict the aroma of a real sample, where relative concentrations, the plant matrix and other volatile compounds also matter; nor do they indicate any effect on the body.

## Why does the terpene profile change?

A study published in 2026 analysed 28 samples collected under different spatial and temporal conditions from six *Cannabis sativa* cultivars, identifying 227 volatile terpenes and 41 full-length genes belonging to the terpene synthase family. The authors observed different patterns according to tissue, developmental stage and cultivar.

The important point is not the number itself, but what it suggests: the volatile profile is the result of a complex biological network. Genetics matter, but they are not the only factor. The developmental stage of the plant and the tissue being analysed also have an effect.

In a 2023 study, commercial samples of two cultivars obtained from genetically identical clones of the same parental material, but grown indoors and outdoors, showed significant differences in terpene profiles. Because the comparison included several environmental differences — including lighting and growing medium — it cannot attribute the effect to a single parameter; it does show, however, that different cultivation conditions can contribute to the observed variability.

Post-harvest handling matters too. A 2024 study on three varieties of *Cannabis sativa* showed that drying method and storage conditions alter the profile of volatile organic compounds and that the initial volatilome does not remain unchanged over time under the tested conditions. Two samples from the same cultivar can therefore diverge even after harvest: the result depends not only on genetics, but also on how the material is dried and stored.
