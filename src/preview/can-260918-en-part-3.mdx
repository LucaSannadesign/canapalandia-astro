## Aroma and terpene profile are not the same thing

One of the most interesting points to emerge from recent research is the gap between chemical analysis and human perception.

A 2025 study developed a sensory lexicon of 25 descriptors and had a human panel evaluate 91 samples. Terpene analyses formed recognisable chemical clusters but predicted the overall sensory character poorly. In type III samples, 43 volatile sulphur compounds were also detected; however, their profile alone did not strongly predict sensory perception either. The most accurate conclusion is therefore that real-world aroma cannot be reduced to a single chemical panel.

This does not make terpene profiling useless. More precisely, it means that a concentration table is not a perfect snapshot of the olfactory experience. The 2025 study is limited to the samples analysed and does not allow every aroma association to be generalised, but it clearly illustrates a practical limitation: using the terpene panel alone as a proxy for aroma can be insufficient. Perceived aroma, plant matrix, relative concentrations and the presence of other compounds interact with one another.

Associations such as “limonene = citrus” or “pinene = resinous” should therefore be read as indicative descriptions of pure molecules or profiles in which they contribute substantially, not as a rule capable of predicting the smell of a real sample on its own.

## How are terpenes analysed?

In the laboratory, volatile compounds are normally studied using chromatographic techniques, often coupled with mass spectrometry. Methods based on gas chromatography and headspace sampling can identify and quantify many compounds within a complex mixture.

These analyses can be useful for comparing batches, checking process consistency or describing a chemical profile. But, as with any laboratory result, the method matters: sample preparation, analytical standards, limits of quantification and measurement conditions all influence the result.

For this reason, a “terpene profile” should not be treated as a universal quality score. It is analytical data that becomes meaningful only when we know how it was obtained and what question it is intended to answer.
