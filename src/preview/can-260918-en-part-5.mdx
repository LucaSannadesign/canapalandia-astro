In 2024, a small double-blind crossover study in 20 adults found that 15 mg of D-limonene administered together with 30 mg of THC reduced some subjective ratings of anxiety and paranoia compared with THC alone. The finding is interesting but limited: the highest-limonene condition involved 12 participants, used isolated compounds and used a THC:limonene ratio that the authors themselves considered poorly representative of normal commercial cannabis flower.

In 2025, by contrast, a study in 19 adults did not find that alpha-pinene, even at doses equal to or higher than those typically present in the plant, reduced THC-induced memory impairment or altered the other major acute effects.

The updated picture is therefore more nuanced than a simple “it exists/it does not exist”: some terpene-cannabinoid interactions have been observed in specific experimental models and in at least one small human study, while other hypotheses have not been confirmed. There is still not enough evidence to turn a product’s terpene profile into an individual clinical prediction or a therapeutic promise.

In practical terms, the presence of myrcene, limonene or other terpenes on a certificate does not allow a reliable prediction of a therapeutic outcome for a person and does not justify health claims.

Caution is especially important when the topic is linked to CBD, medicines or health conditions. In these areas, experimental data, clinical evidence and hypotheses must always be distinguished. For interactions between CBD and medicines, the relevant reference is the specific literature, not the terpene profile: [CBD and drug interactions: what we know](/blog/cbd-interazioni-farmaci-rischi/).

## How to read a terpene profile without being misled

A terpene profile can be useful if it is treated for what it is: a partial chemical description of a sample.

It can help compare batches, document variability, study cultivars and processes, or complement a sensory assessment. It can also offer clues about the origin of some aromatic notes.

When interpretation moves from chemical composition to an effect on a person, specific data are needed on the product, dose, route of administration and outcome being considered.

> ### Four useful questions
> 1. Which sample was analysed, and when?
> 2. Which analytical method was used?
> 3. Does the result describe an actual concentration or only the presence of the compound?
> 4. Does the interpretation remain chemical and sensory, or is it turned without evidence into a promise of an effect?

These questions are more useful than any ranking of the “best terpenes”.
