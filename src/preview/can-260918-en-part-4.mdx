## Can terpenes predict effects?

This is where commercial language tends to run ahead of the evidence.

Online, it is common to find tables linking individual terpenes to effects such as relaxation, energy, concentration or relief. Some of these associations come from pharmacological studies on isolated molecules, cell models or animal models. Automatically transferring them to a cannabis or hemp product, however, requires steps of evidence that are often missing.

An experiment published in 2020 tested five common terpenes — myrcene, alpha-pinene, beta-pinene, beta-caryophyllene and limonene — to determine whether they interacted directly with cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2 or modified the activity of THC, CBD or 2-AG. With the possible exception of a weak beta-caryophyllene interaction with CB2, that study found no evidence supporting direct modulation through those receptors in the experimental system used.

That result does not show that terpenes are biologically inactive and, more importantly, it does not settle the mechanism question. In 2026, a different *in vitro* study using CB1 and CB2 receptors expressed in *Xenopus laevis* oocytes and measured through GIRK channels observed additive or synergistic interactions between THC and certain terpenes. Examples reported included limonene and alpha-pinene at CB1, and beta-caryophyllene and linalool at CB2.

The contrast with the 2020 findings indicates that the outcome also depends on the experimental model and that the receptor mechanism cannot be considered definitively resolved. The conflict-of-interest statement in the 2026 study should also be considered: three of the six authors were reported as employees of Bazelet Medical Cannabis Group, an Israeli medical cannabis producer. This is a transparency point to keep in mind when interpreting the findings, not a sufficient reason to dismiss them.

## What do we really know about the “entourage effect”?

The expression “entourage effect” is used to describe the hypothesis that multiple cannabis components may interact and produce effects different from those of the individual compounds in isolation.

It is a legitimate scientific hypothesis, but it should not be turned into a commercial certainty.

Reviews published in 2024 concluded that the available evidence did not support treating the “entourage effect” as a stable, predictable and clinically demonstrated phenomenon. That conclusion must, however, be read in its time context: the review by André and colleagues, organised according to PRISMA methodology, searched the literature available up to 22 December 2023 and therefore did not include some later human and experimental studies.
